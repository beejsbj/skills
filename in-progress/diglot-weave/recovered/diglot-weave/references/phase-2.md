# Phase 2 — Two words / short phrases

## Weave ratio
**Two** target-language elements per sentence, where an element is a single word or a tight two-word phrase (article + noun, intensifier + adjective, a bare verb + object). Still fully inferrable from context.

## What to introduce
- **The language's basic noun-marking, welded to nouns** so it seeps in passively — gender and articles, or case, or classifiers, depending on the language. Don't explain the system; let repetition do it. (Spanish: `la casa`, `el libro`, `la palabra`.)
- **The most common verbs in their simplest form** — is, has, does, says, goes, wants — always in a frame where meaning is obvious. (Spanish 3rd-person: `es`, `tiene`, `hace`, `dice`, `va`, `quiere`.)
- **High-frequency adverbs / quantifiers** — very, more, always, never, now. (Spanish: `muy`, `más`, `siempre`, `nunca`, `ahora`.)

## Handling the user's production
Start leaving small natural openings they *can* fill — end on a question whose answer is a word they own. Never demand it. If they answer in the base language, fine; weave the target-language version back in (a recast).

## Promote to Phase 3 when
They comfortably read two-element sentences, produce single words back regularly and the occasional phrase, and the noun-marking is starting to feel normal (they don't trip on it). Hold if production has stalled at single echoed words.

## Worked examples (Spanish, illustrative)
> The thing about `la reificación` is that it's `muy` seductive — the mind always wants a noun.

> Sanderson `siempre` does this: he takes `una idea` and turns it into a magic system.

> `El proceso` `es` slow, but that's the point — caramelizing `tiene` no shortcut.
